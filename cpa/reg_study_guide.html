Gross Income

Individual Tax

The Default Rule

All income from whatever source derived is included in gross income unless explicitly excluded by the tax code. When in doubt — it's income.

Always Included

  • Wages, salaries, tips
  • Business income
  • Interest (most)
  • Dividends
  • Alimony (pre-2019 divorces)
  • Rental income
  • Gambling winnings
  • Cancellation of debt (generally)

Key Exclusions

  • Gifts & inheritances
  • Life insurance proceeds (death)
  • Municipal bond interest
  • Workers' compensation
  • Child support received
  • Alimony (post-2018 divorces)
  • Employer health insurance premiums
  • Scholarships (tuition/fees only)
Exam Trap

Constructive receipt: You're taxed when income is available to you, not just when you physically receive it. A check sitting in your mailbox on Dec 31 = income this year, even if you don't cash it until January.

Alimony Rule Change — Know This Cold

Pre-2019 divorce: alimony is income to recipient, deduction for payer. Post-2018 divorce: alimony is NOT income to recipient, NOT deductible for payer. Exam loves to test which year the divorce was finalized.

IRA Early Distributions

Individual Tax

The Base Rule

Distributions from a traditional IRA before age 59½ are subject to a 10% premature distribution penalty tax on top of ordinary income tax. Exceptions exist — see HIM DEAD TED below.

The #1 Exam Trap — Penalty ≠ Income Inclusion

These are two completely separate questions. The penalty being waived does NOT mean the distribution is excluded from gross income. A traditional IRA distribution is always taxable as ordinary income if contributions were previously deducted. An exception only removes the 10% penalty — you still pay income tax.

Exam Question — Williams (Classic Setup)

Mrs. Williams withdraws $10,000 from her traditional IRA on June 12 and uses it toward a first-home purchase on July 1. Contributions were previously deducted.

High-Frequency Exam Topics on IRA Distributions

First-time homebuyer ($10K) and the 120-day rule are the most tested. Know that H, A, and E have dollar caps — the others don't. The trap is always assuming penalty waiver = income exclusion. They never go together for traditional IRAs with deducted contributions.

Alimony Rules

Individual Tax

The TCJA Split — Know Which Year the Divorce Was Finalized

  • Pre-2019 divorce (on or before Dec 31, 2018): Alimony is income to recipient, deductible above-the-line for payer
  • Post-2018 divorce (Jan 1, 2019 or later): Alimony is NOT income to recipient, NOT deductible for payer — treated like child support

Requirements for Pre-2019 Alimony to Qualify (All Must Be Met)

  • Payment must be in cash or its equivalent (not property)
  • Payments must end at the recipient's death — cannot extend beyond it
  • Must be legally required under a written divorce or separation agreement
  • Payer and recipient must not live in the same household
  • Cannot be designated as anything other than alimony (not labeled child support, property settlement, etc.)
  • Spouses cannot file a joint return
Exam Trap

If payments continue after the recipient's death — they're NOT alimony, even if labeled as such. The "ends at death" requirement is the most commonly tested disqualifier. Also watch for payments in property (not cash) — those never qualify.

Exam Question — Pre-2019 Alimony Requirements

For a divorce executed on or before Dec 31, 2018, which conditions must be present for payments to qualify as deductible alimony?

Answer: Both (I) cash or equivalent AND (II) ends at recipient's death. Both are required — not just one.

Child Support vs. Alimony

Child support is never deductible and never income — regardless of divorce date. If a payment is reduced upon a child-related event (turning 18, graduating), the IRS may reclassify it as disguised child support.

Stock Sale Recognition — Trade Date Rule

Individual Tax

The Rule

For listed stock sold on an established securities market, gain or loss is recognized on the trade date — not the settlement date, not the date cash is received, not the date the certificate is delivered. This applies to both cash and accrual basis taxpayers.

Exam Trap — Cash Basis Doesn't Matter Here

Cash basis taxpayers normally recognize income when cash is received. Stock sales are an exception — the trade date rule overrides the cash basis method. The exam specifically tests whether you'll incorrectly apply cash basis logic to stock sales. You won't — it's always the trade date.

Why It Matters at Year-End

If you sell stock on December 30 but settlement isn't until January 3, the gain or loss is recognized in the current year — because the trade date is December 30. The settlement date (when cash and shares actually change hands) is irrelevant for tax recognition purposes.

Exam Question

For a cash basis taxpayer, gain or loss on a year-end sale of listed stock arises on the trade date.

Answer: Trade date — always. Not settlement date, not receipt of cash, not delivery of certificate.

The Three Wrong Answers and Why They're Wrong

Settlement date — when shares/cash actually exchange, but not the recognition date. Date of cash receipt — would apply under normal cash basis rules but stock is an exception. Date of certificate delivery — irrelevant for listed securities.

Annuity Taxation

Individual Tax

The Core Concept

Each annuity payment has two parts: a nontaxable return of capital (getting your own money back) and a taxable income portion (the earnings). You only pay tax on the excess over your original investment.

Fixed-Period Annuity Formula

When payments run for a set number of years (not for life), the math is straightforward:

Return of capital per payment = Investment ÷ Number of payments
Taxable income per payment = Annual payment − Return of capital
Exam Question — $100K Annuity

Retiree invests $100,000 in annuity paying $12,000/year for 10 years.

Return of capital: $100,000 ÷ 10 years = $10,000 per payment (nontaxable)

Taxable income: $12,000 − $10,000 = $2,000 included in gross income

Exam Traps

$12,000 (full payment) — wrong. You already paid tax on the $100K investment, so getting it back isn't income.

$10,000 (return of capital) — wrong. That's the nontaxable part, not the taxable part. Easy to flip these under pressure.

$0 — wrong. There IS income — the $2,000 above your basis recovery.

What Happens After 10 Years?

Once you've fully recovered your $100,000 investment (after 10 payments), 100% of each subsequent payment is taxable — there's no more basis left to recover. If the annuity continues past the expected period, everything is income.

Life Annuity vs. Fixed-Period Annuity

For a life annuity, you use the exclusion ratio = Investment ÷ Expected total return (investment × expected payments based on IRS life expectancy tables). Fixed-period is simpler — just divide by the number of years. REG most commonly tests fixed-period.

Social Security Benefits

Individual Tax

The Key Number — 85% Maximum

85% is the maximum amount of Social Security benefits that can be included in gross income. Never 100%. The actual amount depends on modified AGI — higher earners get taxed on up to 85%, lower earners pay less or nothing.

Modified AGI Formula

Modified AGI = AGI + tax-exempt interest + 50% of SS benefits
Filing Status Modified AGI % of SS Taxable

Single Over $34,000 Up to 85%
Married Filing Jointly Under $32,000 0%
Married Filing Jointly $32,000 – $44,000 Up to 50%
Married Filing Jointly Over $44,000 Up to 85%
Exam Traps

100% taxable — never. 85% is the hard ceiling.

Never taxable — wrong. High earners pay tax on up to 85%.

50% is the max — was true before 1994, no longer. 85% is the current max. The exam may use 50% as a trap since it appears in the modified AGI formula.

Why 50% Shows Up in the Formula but Isn't the Max

You add 50% of SS benefits to AGI to calculate modified AGI — that's just how the threshold test works. But the actual taxable amount can be up to 85% once you're above the upper threshold. Don't confuse the formula ingredient with the maximum inclusion rate.

Decedent's Final Tax Return

Individual Tax

Due Date Rule

The final income tax return of a decedent is due on the same date it would have been due if the taxpayer were still alive — regardless of when during the year death occurred. For a calendar-year taxpayer, that's always April 15 of the year following death.

What Goes on the Final Return vs. the Estate Return

  • Final 1040 (decedent's return): Income earned or received by the taxpayer before the date of death
  • Form 1041 (estate's return): Income received by the estate after the date of death
Exam Q1 — Due Date (Daisy Dunn)

Daisy dies March 1, Year 1. Earned $20,000 in wages + $500 interest before death. Estate receives $1,500 interest after death.

Final return due: April 15, Year 2 — same as if she were alive. Death date doesn't change the deadline.

Exam Q2 — Taxable Gross Income (Same Daisy Dunn Facts)

Cash-basis taxpayer. Wages $20,000 + interest $500 received before death. Estate receives $1,500 after death.

Gross income on final 1040: $20,500 ($20,000 wages + $500 interest — both earned and received before death).

The $1,500 is excluded from the final 1040 — it was received by the estate after death and goes on Form 1041.

Cash basis matters here: income is recognized when received. Since Daisy received the $500 interest before death, it's on her return. The $1,500 was received by the estate — different taxpayer, different return.

Exam Traps

April 15, Year 1 — wrong. That would only be correct if she died in Year 0. The return covers Year 1 income, so it's due April 15 Year 2.

March 1, Year 2 — wrong. Death date has nothing to do with the filing deadline.

Including post-death income on the 1040 — wrong. The $1,500 received by the estate belongs on Form 1041, not her final return.

One-Line Rule to Memorize

Death doesn't change the filing deadline. April 15 following the year of death — always. The only thing that changes is what income is included (pre-death only) and that a surviving spouse or executor signs the return.

Employer Educational Assistance — §127

Individual Tax

The Rule

Up to $5,250 per year paid by an employer for an employee's educational expenses is excludable from gross income. Anything above $5,250 is taxable as ordinary wages.

Exam Question — $8,000 Reimbursement

Employer reimburses employee $8,000 for tuition. How much is taxable?

Nontaxable: $5,250 (excluded under §127)

Taxable: $2,750 ($8,000 − $5,250 = included in gross income as wages)

Exam Trap

The exam will give you a total reimbursement and make you split it. The answer is never $0 (if over $5,250) and never the full amount. Always apply the $5,250 cap and tax the rest.

Number to Memorize

$5,250 — the only number that matters here. Per year, per employee, regardless of type of education.

Scholarship Exclusion

Individual Tax

The Rule

Scholarships for a degree-seeking student are excludable from gross income — but only for amounts spent on tuition, fees, books, and required supplies. Anything else (room, board, travel, personal) is taxable.

Excluded (Nontaxable)

  • Tuition
  • Enrollment fees
  • Books
  • Required supplies and equipment

Taxable

  • Room and board
  • Travel
  • Research / clerical help
  • Any amount retained (not spent on qualifying expenses)
Exam Question — $15,000 Scholarship

Student receives $15,000 scholarship. Spends $10,000 on tuition, $2,000 on books and supplies, $3,000 on room and board.

Nontaxable: $12,000 ($10,000 tuition + $2,000 books/supplies)

Taxable: $3,000 (room and board — not a qualifying expense)

Exam Trap — Books Are Excluded, Room & Board Is Not

The common wrong answer is excluding only tuition and fees. Books and required supplies ARE also excluded — don't forget them. Room and board is always taxable, no exceptions.

Degree-Seeking Requirement

The exclusion only applies to degree-seeking students. Scholarships to non-degree students (e.g., for a standalone certificate program at a non-degree institution) are generally fully taxable.

Individual Tax Deductions

Individual Tax

Two Types — Know the Difference

Above-the-line deductions reduce AGI directly and are available to everyone — you do NOT need to itemize. Below-the-line (itemized) deductions only help if the total exceeds the standard deduction.

Standard Deduction (2025)Amount
Single / MFS$15,000
Married Filing Jointly$30,000
Head of Household$22,500

Above-the-Line Deductions (No Itemizing Required)

  • Student loan interest — up to $2,500; phases out at higher income
  • 50% of self-employment tax — see Topic 11
  • HSA contributions — up to annual limits
  • Traditional IRA contributions — up to $7,000 ($8,000 if 50+); income limits apply if covered by employer plan
  • Alimony paid — pre-2019 divorces only
  • Self-employed health insurance — 100% of premiums
  • Self-employed retirement contributions — SEP, SIMPLE, Solo 401(k)

Below-the-Line (Itemized) Deductions

  • Mortgage interest — on loans up to $750,000 (post-2017 loans)
  • State and local taxes (SALT) — capped at $10,000 (combined income + property taxes)
  • Charitable contributions — cash gifts up to 60% of AGI; property gifts up to 30%
  • Medical expenses — only the amount exceeding 7.5% of AGI
  • Casualty losses — only from federally declared disasters (post-TCJA)
Post-TCJA — Gone Since 2018

These were eliminated by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act and are no longer deductible: unreimbursed employee business expenses, tax preparation fees, investment advisory fees, and all other miscellaneous itemized deductions that used to be subject to the 2% of AGI floor.

Exam Question — Medical Expense Threshold

Taxpayer has AGI of $80,000 and unreimbursed medical expenses of $7,000. How much is deductible?

7.5% floor: $80,000 × 7.5% = $6,000

Deductible amount: $7,000 − $6,000 = $1,000

Exam Strategy — Above vs. Below the Line

Above-the-line deductions are almost always better — they reduce AGI which can unlock other benefits. The exam will often ask whether a specific deduction is above or below the line. Student loan interest, IRA contributions, and SE tax are the most commonly tested above-the-line items.

Self-Employment Tax — The Starting Point

Self-employed individuals pay SE tax of 15.3% on net SE income (12.4% Social Security + 2.9% Medicare). The SS portion applies only up to the wage base ($176,100 for 2025). Medicare is unlimited. As a partial offset, 50% of SE tax is deductible above-the-line.

SE tax is not applied to 100% of net income — it's applied to 92.35% of net income. The 7.65% reduction accounts for the employer-equivalent half of FICA that a W-2 employee's employer pays separately (100% − 7.65% = 92.35%). Since self-employed individuals pay both halves themselves, the IRS discounts the base to avoid taxing that employer portion twice.

SE Tax Deduction — Quick Example

Self-employed taxpayer has net SE income of $100,000.

SE tax = $100,000 × 92.35% (reduces base by employer-equivalent 7.65%) × 15.3% = ~$14,130.

Above-the-line deduction: $7,065 (50% of $14,130) — reduces AGI before any other deductions.

Self-Employed Health Insurance

Self-employed individuals can deduct 100% of health insurance premiums paid for themselves, their spouse, and dependents — above-the-line. Limit: deduction cannot exceed net SE income. Also cannot use this deduction for any month the taxpayer was eligible to participate in an employer-subsidized plan (e.g., through a spouse's job).

Self-Employed Retirement Plans

  • SEP-IRA: Up to the lesser of 25% of net SE compensation or $70,000 (2025) — above-the-line
  • SIMPLE IRA: Employee contributions up to $16,500 (2025); employer match required
  • Solo 401(k): Combines employee + employer contributions; higher limit than SEP for lower incomes

Business Expenses — Schedule C

Ordinary and necessary business expenses reduce SE income on Schedule C before SE tax is calculated. Common deductible items:

  • Advertising, office supplies, professional fees, software
  • Home office: Simplified = $5/sq ft, max 300 sq ft ($1,500 max). Must be used regularly and exclusively for business
  • Vehicle: Standard mileage rate (67¢/mile for 2024) or actual expenses — keep a log
  • Business meals: 50% deductible

§199A Qualified Business Income (QBI) Deduction

Self-employed individuals (and pass-through entity owners) can deduct up to 20% of qualified business income. Subject to income phase-outs and W-2 wage limitations for higher earners. Not available for specified service trades/businesses (SSTBs) — like law, health, consulting — above the income threshold.

Where Does 92.35% Come From?

Self-employed individuals pay both the employee and employer halves of FICA (15.3% total). To avoid taxing the "employer half" twice, the IRS lets you reduce your net SE income by 7.65% before calculating SE tax:

100% − 7.65% = 92.35%

The 7.65% represents the employer's share of FICA — the half a W-2 employee never sees because the employer pays it separately. Since a self-employed person pays both halves, the 92.35% factor on Schedule SE is the IRS's way of giving you the equivalent deduction. The resulting SE tax is then computed on that reduced base, and separately 50% of the SE tax computed is deductible above-the-line.

Full SE Tax Flow — Step by Step

Step 1 — Schedule C Net Profit
Revenue minus ordinary/necessary business expenses = net SE income. This is your starting number for everything.
Step 2 — Net Earnings from Self-Employment
Net profit × 92.35% = amount subject to SE tax. (The 7.65% reduction accounts for the employer-equivalent half of FICA — see 92.35% note above.)
Step 3 — Self-Employment Tax
Net earnings × 15.3% (12.4% SS + 2.9% Medicare) = SE tax owed. Computed on Schedule SE.
Step 4 — Income Tax Starts with Full Net Profit
For income tax purposes you begin with the full Schedule C net profit — NOT the 92.35%-reduced amount. The 92.35% reduction only applies to the SE tax calculation.
Step 5 — Above-the-Line Deduction (50% of SE Tax)
Deduct 50% of SE tax directly on Form 1040 as an above-the-line adjustment. This reduces AGI. It is NOT taken from Schedule C — it comes off the 1040 after Schedule C is complete.
Step 6 — AGI → Standard/Itemized Deduction → Taxable Income
From AGI, subtract either the standard deduction or itemized deductions to arrive at taxable income.
Step 7 — Apply Income Tax Rates
Apply the ordinary income tax brackets to taxable income. SE tax and income tax are two separate taxes — both are owed, computed independently, then added together on the return.

Exam Trap — Schedule C vs. Above-the-Line (Tested Directly)

Schedule C is for business operating expenses only — things that run the business. Self-employed personal benefits go on the 1040 as above-the-line adjustments, NOT on Schedule C.

Does NOT go on Schedule C: health insurance for self/family, 50% of SE tax, SEP-IRA contributions.

Does go on Schedule C: employee salaries, depreciation on office equipment, business taxes, rent, supplies — anything that operates the business itself.

Classic exam question: attorney sole proprietor — health insurance for him and his family is NOT a Schedule C deduction. It's a 1040 above-the-line adjustment. The paralegal's salary and computer depreciation ARE Schedule C.

Self-Employed vs. Employee — Key Difference

Employees can no longer deduct unreimbursed business expenses (eliminated post-TCJA). Self-employed individuals still deduct all ordinary and necessary business expenses on Schedule C. This is a major tax advantage of self-employment the exam may contrast.

Hobby Loss Rules

Deductions

The Core Rule (Post-TCJA — Know This Cold)

If an activity is not engaged in for profit (a hobby), the income is fully taxable and the expenses are completely nondeductible. Post-TCJA (2018+), hobby expenses lost their last remaining deduction path — the misc. itemized deduction subject to the 2% AGI floor was eliminated. There is no offset.

Hobby (Not for Profit)

  • Income → fully taxable
  • Expenses → zero deduction
  • Net result: always an increase in taxable income

Business (Profit Intent)

  • Income → taxable
  • Expenses → fully deductible on Schedule C
  • Net loss can offset other income

How the IRS Determines Profit Intent — Presumption Rule

  • Activity is presumed to be a business if it shows a profit in at least 3 of the last 5 consecutive years
  • For horse breeding/racing/training/showing: 2 of 7 years
  • Failing the test doesn't automatically make it a hobby — it just shifts the burden of proof to the taxpayer
Exam Question — Jennifer's Poodles

Jennifer raises poodles as a hobby (no profit intent). Prize money: $1,000. Expenses: $1,300 (food $300, vet $800, travel $200).

Taxable income effect: +$1,000 increase.

The $1,000 prize money is fully included in gross income. The $1,300 in expenses are nondeductible — post-TCJA, hobby expenses have no deduction path. The loss does not offset the income or anything else.

Exam Trap — The Intuitive Wrong Answer

It feels like $1,000 income − $1,000 of expenses = $0, or $1,000 − $1,300 = −$300 decrease. Both are wrong. Post-TCJA, hobby expenses are completely disallowed. You include 100% of the income and deduct none of the expenses. The answer is always the full income amount as an increase.

Pre-TCJA vs. Post-TCJA — What Changed

Before 2018, hobby expenses were deductible as miscellaneous itemized deductions to the extent of hobby income (subject to the 2% AGI floor). That deduction was eliminated by TCJA. Exam questions may be written under either regime — check the year in the question. If post-2017, expenses are gone entirely.

Mixed-Use Property — Personal/Rental Residence

Deductions

The Classification Test — Two Conditions Must Both Be Met

A property is treated as a personal/rental residence (mixed-use) if:

  • Rented for more than 14 days, AND
  • Personal use exceeds the greater of: (1) 14 days, or (2) 10% of rental days
Exam Question — Adams (Classic Setup)

Adams rents second residence 200 days, uses personally 50 days.

Step 1 — Is it rented > 14 days? Yes (200 days). ✓

Step 2 — Personal use > greater of 14 days or 10% of rental days?
10% of 200 rental days = 20 days. Personal use = 50 days > 20 days. ✓

Result: Personal/rental residence. Expenses must be prorated between personal and rental use.

Consequences of Personal/Rental Classification

  • Expenses must be prorated — rental vs. personal based on days used
  • Rental losses cannot be deducted — rental expenses are limited to rental income (no net loss allowed)
  • Depreciation IS allowed — but only on the rental portion
  • Mortgage interest and taxes — must be split; rental portion on Schedule E, personal portion on Schedule A

Expense Proration Formula

Rental % = Rental days ÷ (Rental days + Personal days)
Adams: 200 ÷ (200 + 50) = 200 ÷ 250 = 80% rental / 20% personal
Exam Traps — All Four Wrong Answers

Rental loss is deductible — wrong. Personal/rental residence rules cap rental deductions at rental income. No net loss.

Depreciation not allowed under any circumstances — wrong. Depreciation IS allowed on the rental portion. It's just prorated.

All mortgage interest and taxes deducted against rental income — wrong. They must be split between personal (Schedule A) and rental (Schedule E) based on days used.

Trap #1 — Advance Rent Is Taxable Immediately

Individual Tax

The Rule — One of the Rare Full Exclusions in the Code

If a taxpayer rents their primary residence for 14 days or fewer during the year, the rental income is completely excluded from gross income — no matter how much is received. In exchange, no rental expenses are deductible. Mortgage interest and property taxes remain deductible as itemized deductions on Schedule A (as they would be anyway for a primary home).

The Core Exam Trap — The Answer Is $0, Not $7,500 or $8,500

The intuitive approach is to include $10,000 income and subtract some or all expenses: $10,000 − $1,500 = $8,500, or $10,000 − $2,500 = $7,500. Both are wrong. Under the ≤14-day rule, the taxpayer cannot deduct advertising or commissions — those are rental expenses, and the rule eliminates all rental deductions. S Corporation Pass-Through Income

Business Tax

The Core Rule — Income Is Taxed at the Shareholder Level

An S corporation pays no federal income tax at the entity level. Instead, income and losses flow through to shareholders based on their pro rata ownership percentage and are included in gross income in the year earned — regardless of whether any distributions are made. Distributions are a separate question from income inclusion.

Income Inclusion

  • Always based on pro rata share of S corp income
  • Taxable in the year earned by the corporation
  • Regardless of actual distributions made
  • Increases the shareholder's basis

Distributions

  • Not separately taxable if basis is sufficient
  • Reduce basis (not income)
  • Taxable only to the extent they exceed adjusted basis
  • Never "double counted" with allocated income

Exam Question — Miyasyke Inc. (5 Equal Shareholders)

5 equal shareholders. S corp taxable income: $75,000. Distribution per shareholder: $32,000. Beginning basis per shareholder: $100,000.

Step 1 — Pro rata income share: $75,000 ÷ 5 = $15,000 included in each shareholder's gross income.

Step 2 — Is the $32,000 distribution taxable?

Beginning basis: $100,000
+ Income allocated: +$15,000
Adjusted basis: $115,000
− Distribution: −$32,000
Ending basis: $83,000

Basis ($115,000) exceeds distribution ($32,000) → distribution is NOT taxable. It simply reduces basis.

Answer: $15,000 included in gross income.

Trap #1 — $47,000 ($15,000 + $32,000) — Double Counting

The most common wrong answer is to add pro rata income AND the full distribution: $15,000 + $32,000 = $47,000. This is double counting. Distributions are not separately taxable when basis covers them. You already paid tax on the income when it was allocated — the distribution is just getting your own money back.

Trap #2 — $32,000 (Distribution Only) — Ignores Pass-Through Rule

Some answers treat the distribution as the taxable amount. Wrong for two reasons: (1) distributions aren't income here because basis exceeds the distribution, and (2) even if they were, the income allocation would still be the right measure. Pro rata income share — not distribution — is what you include in gross income.

Trap #3 — $0 (Distributions Don't Trigger Tax, So Nothing?) — Forgets Income Flows Through

It's true the distribution is nontaxable. But income still flows through. Each shareholder must report their $15,000 pro rata share whether or not any distribution is made. $0 would only be correct if the S corp had no income — the distribution amount is irrelevant to income inclusion.

S Corp vs. C Corp — The Key Distinction the Exam Tests

A C corporation pays entity-level tax; shareholders only pay tax on dividends received (double taxation). Partnership Pass-Through Income

Business Tax

The Core Rule — Two Categories of Income

A partnership pays no entity-level tax. Income flows through to partners based on their partnership percentage. But not all income is treated the same — the exam requires you to distinguish:

  • Ordinary income (non-separately stated) — reported on Schedule E; partner's share flows through at their ordinary income rate
  • Separately stated items — capital gains/losses, §1231 gains, charitable contributions, §179 expense — each must be reported separately because the tax treatment depends on the partner's individual situation

Ordinary Income (Non-Separately Stated)

  • Net operating revenue minus ordinary expenses
  • Flows through as one number per partner
  • Partner's share = ownership % × partnership ordinary income

Separately Stated Items

  • Long-term / short-term capital gains & losses
  • §1231 gains and losses
  • Charitable contributions
  • §179 deduction
  • Tax-exempt income

Partnership Basis Loss Limitation & Capital Gain/Loss Netting

Business Tax

Rule 1 — Partnership Losses Are Limited to Basis

A partner can only deduct their share of partnership losses to the extent of their tax basis in the partnership interest. Any losses in excess of basis are suspended and carry forward to future years — they become deductible only when the partner's basis is restored (through future income allocations, contributions, or increased debt share).

Rule 2 — Capital Gains and Losses Must Be Netted

All capital gains and losses for the year are netted together before determining the tax result. Short-term and long-term are netted within their own buckets first, then cross-netted if one bucket is positive and the other is negative. Individual taxpayers may deduct up to $3,000 of net capital losses per year against ordinary income; excess carries forward indefinitely.

Exam Question — AGI Computation with Basis Limitation

Taxpayer: Salary $122,000 | Capital gain dividends (LTCG) $3,700 | Partnership STCL $(6,300) | Capital contribution (basis) $2,750 — no other basis adjustments.

Step 1 — Apply basis limitation to partnership loss:
Partner's basis = $2,750 (capital contribution only). STCL allowed = $2,750. Remaining $3,550 ($6,300 − $2,750) is suspended — carries forward to a future year.

Step 2 — Net capital gains and losses:
LTCG: $3,700 | STCL (allowed): $(2,750) → Net LTCG = $950. Since the result is a net gain, the $3,000 annual cap on capital loss deductions does not apply here.

Salary: $122,000
LTCG dividends: +$3,700
STCL (limited to basis): −$2,750
Net capital gain: $950
AGI: $122,950
Trap #1 — $119,400 (Deducting the Full $6,300 Loss)

If you ignore the basis limitation and allow the full $6,300 STCL: $122,000 + $3,700 − $6,300 = $119,400. Wrong. The partner's basis is only $2,750. The excess $3,550 loss is suspended — it cannot be deducted this year regardless of the $3,000 annual cap.

Trap #2 — $122,000 (Ignoring Capital Items Entirely)

The $3,700 LTCG from capital gain dividends is gross income — it cannot be ignored. Even if you correctly suspend the full partnership loss, the capital gain dividends still flow into AGI. Salary alone ($122,000) understates AGI.

Trap #3 — Confusing the $3,000 Cap with the Basis Limitation

These are two completely separate rules applied in sequence:
(1) Basis limitation first — reduces the allowable loss from $6,300 to $2,750.
(2) Capital netting second — $3,700 LTCG − $2,750 STCL = $950 net gain → $3,000 cap is irrelevant because the net result is a gain, not a loss.
The $3,000 cap only matters when, after all netting, you still have a net capital loss to deduct against ordinary income.

Suspended Loss — What Restores Basis in Future Years

  • Additional capital contributions by the partner
  • Pro rata share of future partnership income allocated to the partner
  • Increased share of partnership liabilities (recourse or nonrecourse debt)

Once basis is restored, the suspended $3,550 STCL can be deducted — subject to the same capital netting and $3,000 cap rules that apply in that future year.

Why 20%? — The Circular Calculation Explained

The IRS defines the SEP-IRA limit as 25% of "net compensation" — but for self-employed individuals, net compensation is defined as net SE income after deducting the SEP-IRA contribution itself. This creates a circular calculation. The IRS resolves it with a simplified formula:

Effective rate = 25% ÷ (1 + 25%) = 20%

So for self-employed: (Net SE income − 50% of SE tax) × 20% is the correct formula. Using 25% directly overstates the deduction. The exam will give you the net income and SE tax and expect you to apply 20% — not 25%.

$10,000 = $50,000 × 20% — forgot to subtract 50% of SE tax first
$8,587 = ($50,000 − $7,064) × 20% — subtracted full SE tax instead of 50%
$70,000 = the cap, not the calculation — only correct if net earnings were $350,000+
Trap — "Net Compensation" for SEP-IRA Is Not Schedule C Net Profit

The exam will give you Schedule C net profit and expect you to apply 25%. But the correct base is net compensation = Schedule C net profit minus 50% of SE tax. Using gross Schedule C income overstates the contribution limit. The SE tax deduction comes first, then you apply 25% to what's left.

Student Loan Interest Deduction

Individual Tax

The Core Rules

  • Duration: Deductible every year interest is paid — no time limit. As long as you're paying qualified student loan interest, you can deduct it.
  • Maximum: Up to $2,500 per year regardless of how much interest was actually paid
  • Type: Above-the-line deduction — reduces AGI without needing to itemize
  • Loan must be: A qualified education loan used to pay for tuition, fees, books, room and board at an eligible institution
  • MFS restriction: Married taxpayers must file jointly to claim the deduction — married filing separately = no deduction
The Core Exam Trap — There Is No Year Limit

The exam offers 5 years, 10 years, and current year only as wrong answers. None of these are correct. There is no time cap on the student loan interest deduction. You deduct it every year you pay interest on a qualified loan, period. The only limit is the $2,500 annual maximum and the income phase-out — not a number of years.

Phase-Out — Income Limits (2025)

The deduction phases out as MAGI increases:

Single / HOH: Phase-out $85,000 – $100,000 MAGI
Married Filing Jointly: Phase-out $175,000 – $205,000 MAGI
Above the top of the range: No deduction allowed

Who CAN Deduct

  • Taxpayer legally obligated to repay the loan
  • Single filers under $100,000 MAGI
  • MFJ filers under $205,000 MAGI
  • Loan used for qualified higher education expenses

Who CANNOT Deduct

  • Married Filing Separately — always disqualified
  • Anyone claimed as a dependent on another return
  • Income above the phase-out ceiling
  • Loans from related parties (family members)
Exam Question — Duration

An individual starts paying student loan interest in the current year. How many years may the individual deduct a portion of the student loan interest?

Answer: For the duration of time that interest is paid — no year cap. The deduction is available every year up to $2,500, as long as interest is being paid on a qualified loan and income is within the phase-out range.

Exam Question — $1,000 Interest, $25,000 MAGI

Unmarried individual, MAGI $25,000, paid $1,000 interest on a qualified education loan entered into July 1 of the current year.

Answer: $1,000 deduction to arrive at AGI (above-the-line).

MAGI of $25,000 is well below the $85,000 single phase-out threshold — no reduction. Full $1,000 is deductible above-the-line. July 1 start date is a red herring — there is no proration rule based on when the loan originated. You deduct whatever interest was actually paid during the year.

B ($1,000 itemized) — wrong. Student loan interest is above-the-line, not itemized. Above-the-line is always better — it reduces AGI regardless of whether you take the standard deduction.

C (nondeductible personal interest) — wrong. Personal interest (credit cards, car loans) is nondeductible, but qualified education loan interest has its own specific above-the-line deduction.

D ($500) — wrong. There is no half-year proration. The loan started July 1 but you deduct actual interest paid — $1,000 — not a time-prorated amount.
$2,500 Is the Max — Not the Deduction

If you paid $1,800 in student loan interest, you deduct $1,800. If you paid $4,000, you deduct $2,500. The $2,500 is a ceiling, not a flat amount. The exam may give you an interest amount under $2,500 and wait for you to cap it incorrectly.

Charitable Contributions

Deductions

Step 1 — Start with AGI

All charitable contribution limits are a percentage of Adjusted Gross Income (AGI). The category of donation determines which percentage cap applies. Excess above the cap carries forward 5 years.

Donation TypePublic Charity LimitPrivate Foundation Limit

Why LTCG Property Gets the Lowest Limit

  • You deduct full FMV — not your cost basis
  • You never pay capital gains on the appreciation
  • Double benefit = lower cap to compensate
  • Example: bought stock at $5K, worth $30K → deduct $30K AND avoid tax on $25K gain

Carryforward Rule

  • Excess above the annual cap carries forward 5 years only — then expires
  • No carryback — excess cannot go to prior years
  • Example: $100K AGI, donate $70K cash → deduct $60K now, $10K carries forward up to 5 years
  • Each category's carryforward retains its original character and limit

Critical Distinction — 60% vs. 50% Overall Ceiling

  • Cash only: 60% of AGI limit applies
  • Mixed donations (any property in the mix): 50% of AGI is the overall ceiling for all contributions combined

The 60% cash limit effectively becomes 50% the moment you add any property to the mix. The exam exploits this by showing you a 60% and a 30% cap and waiting for you to add them.

The Real Coordination Rule — "Lesser Of" for LTCG Property

Deductions are applied in order: Cash → Ordinary income property → LTCG property. Each tier fills from the same 50% AGI overall ceiling. The LTCG deduction is the lesser of two numbers:

  • A: 30% × AGI (the direct LTCG cap)
  • B: 50% × AGI minus all prior deductions already taken (cash + ordinary property)

Take whichever is lower. If prior deductions already consumed the full 50% ceiling, LTCG gets $0 this year — carryforward only.

Acquisition vs. Home Equity Indebtedness

Deductions

The Only Question That Matters: What Was the Money Used For?

The loan label ("mortgage," "HELOC," "home equity loan") is irrelevant. Post-TCJA, deductibility of mortgage-related interest turns entirely on whether the loan proceeds were used to buy, build, or substantially improve a qualified residence.

Acquisition Indebtedness

  • Debt used to buy, build, or substantially improve a qualified residence
  • Interest is deductible on Schedule A
  • Limit: loans up to $750K (post-2017 loans); $1M if originated pre-2018
  • Covers primary residence + one second home

Examples: original mortgage · construction loan · loan to add a second floor · major kitchen remodel financed separately

Home Equity Indebtedness

  • Debt secured by the home but NOT used to buy/build/improve it
  • Post-TCJA: interest is not deductible
  • The house is just collateral — the money went elsewhere

Examples: HELOC used for vacation · HELOC to pay credit cards · home equity loan to buy a car

The TCJA Twist — HELOCs Can Be Acquisition Debt

A HELOC is not automatically "home equity debt" for deductibility purposes. If the HELOC proceeds are used to substantially improve the home that secures the loan, the IRS treats the interest as acquisition debt — deductible. The loan product name doesn't control. Use of funds does.

Kiddie Tax — Net Unearned Income

Tax Computations & Credits

The Rule

A dependent child's net unearned income is taxed at the parents' marginal tax rate — not the child's lower rate. This prevents high-income parents from shifting investment income to their children to exploit lower brackets.

Who It Applies To

  • Child is under age 18 at year-end — kiddie tax applies
  • Age 18: applies if unearned income exceeds earned income
  • Ages 19–23 (full-time student): same as age 18 rule
  • REG exam default: child under 18 → kiddie tax applies

The Formula — Three Lines

  • Total unearned income (interest, dividends, capital gains)
  • Less: standard deduction for dependents ($1,350)
  • Less: threshold taxed at child's own rate ($1,350)
  • = Net unearned income taxed at parents' marginal rate
Example — Chris (Becker)

Facts: Chris, age 5, has $3,000 of interest income and no earned income. Standard deduction for dependents = $1,350.

$3,000 unearned income
− $1,350 standard deduction for dependents
− $1,350 taxed at Chris's marginal rate
= $300 taxed at parents' marginal rate

The remaining $1,350 is taxed at Chris's (the child's) rate. The standard deduction wipes out the first $1,350 entirely.

ComponentAmountTaxed At
Standard deduction for dependent $1,350 Not taxed (deducted)
Child's rate threshold $1,350 Child's marginal rate
Net unearned income $300 Parents' marginal rate
Total unearned income $3,000
Trap #1 — The Answer Is NOT All $3,000 at Parents' Rate

The exam gives you full unearned income and waits for you to choose "all of it" (Option A = $3,000). Wrong. Two reductions apply first: the dependent standard deduction AND the child's own rate threshold. Only the remainder hits the parents' rate.

Trap #2 — The Two $1,350s Are Different Things

The first $1,350 is the standard deduction — it eliminates income entirely (no tax). The second $1,350 is still taxed — just at the child's rate, not the parents'. Don't confuse them. Total reduction to the parents'-rate amount = $2,700, but only $1,350 is actually tax-free.

Trap #3 — Earned Income Is NOT Subject to Kiddie Tax

Wages, tips, and other earned income a child receives are taxed at the child's own rate — always. Kiddie tax only reaches unearned income (interest, dividends, capital gains). If Chris had $3,000 of wages instead of interest, none of it hits the parents' rate.

Numbers to Memorize (2024)

Both thresholds are $1,350 for 2024. Combined = $2,700. Any unearned income above $2,700 is taxed at parents' rate. Below $2,700 → some combination of no tax and child's rate.

The Rule

When prior year AGI exceeds $150,000, the prior-year safe harbor jumps from 100% to 110%. The exam will give you an AGI over $150K and offer 100% as a choice — it's not enough. Baker at $160K must use 110%.

Trap #2 — You Only Need One Safe Harbor, Not Both

Meeting either option avoids the penalty. You don't have to satisfy both. The exam question here asks which ones qualify — the answer is both, because each independently provides safe harbor. In practice, you'd pick whichever is smaller.

Trap #3 — Prior Year Must Cover 12 Months

The prior-year safe harbor (100%/110%) only applies if the prior year's return covered a full 12-month period. A short-year return (e.g., first year of a new entity, or a part-year individual return) doesn't count. The question will flag this — if prior year was less than 12 months, Option 1 (90% of current year) is the only available safe harbor.

The Number to Memorize

$150,000 AGI is the trip wire. At or below → 100% of prior year. Above → 110% of prior year. The 90% current-year option is always available regardless of AGI.

Net Investment Income (NII) Tax

Tax Computations & Credits

The Formula — Two Numbers, Take the Lesser

The NII tax = 3.8% × the lesser of:
(1) The taxpayer's net investment income, OR
(2) The excess of modified AGI over the threshold amount

The "lesser of" logic caps the tax at whichever is smaller — the actual NII earned, or the income above the threshold. You only pay on the overlap.

Filing StatusMAGI Threshold
Single / Head of Household$200,000
Married Filing Jointly$250,000
Married Filing Separately$125,000

What Counts as Net Investment Income

  • Interest, dividends, annuities, royalties
  • Capital gains (net)
  • Passive activity income (rental income from passive activities)
  • Does NOT include: wages, self-employment income, active business income, tax-exempt interest (muni bonds), distributions from qualified retirement plans
Example — How the Lesser Of Works

Facts: Single taxpayer. MAGI = $220,000. NII = $15,000. Threshold = $200,000.

(1) NII = $15,000
(2) Excess MAGI over threshold = $220,000 − $200,000 = $20,000
Lesser = $15,000
NII tax = $15,000 × 3.8% = $570

If NII were $25,000 instead, the lesser would be $20,000 (the excess MAGI), and the tax = $20,000 × 3.8% = $760.

Trap #1 — Lesser, Not Greater

The exam will offer "greater of" as a distractor (Choice C in the Becker question). The tax is always the lesser of the two amounts. "Greater of" would mean you always pay on the larger number — that's not how it works. The lesser-of rule limits the tax to the actual overlap between NII and above-threshold income.

Trap #2 — Rate Is 3.8%, Not 2.8%

The NII surtax rate is 3.8%. The exam places 2.8% as a distractor in both "lesser" and "greater" combinations. 3.8 is the only correct rate — there is no 2.8% in federal tax law.

Trap #3 — This Is a Surtax on Top of Regular Income Tax

The NII tax is an additional 3.8% Medicare surtax — it does not replace the regular income tax on investment income. A taxpayer in the 20% long-term capital gains bracket pays 20% + 3.8% = 23.8% on NII above the threshold.

Trap #4 — Wages and SE Income Are Excluded from NII

The NII tax only hits investment-type income. Wages and self-employment income are subject to the separate 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax (not the 3.8% NII tax). The exam may describe a high earner with both wages and investment income — only the investment piece goes into the NII calculation.

The Two Numbers to Memorize

3.8% rate · lesser of NII or excess MAGI over threshold. Everything else (thresholds, what counts as NII) the exam will give you or test with obvious distractors.

Business Law

The Rule — Legal Sufficiency

Consideration must be legally sufficient — meaning the law recognizes it as valid consideration. That's the only real requirement. Courts do not ask whether it's fair, equal, or exchanged at the same moment.

What Legal Sufficiency Means

  • A bargained-for exchange — each side gives something to get something
  • A benefit to the promisor OR a detriment to the promisee
  • Must be something the party was not already legally obligated to do

What Courts Do NOT Require

  • Fair or reasonable value
  • Equal value on both sides
  • Simultaneous exchange
  • Written form (for most contracts)

Three Things That Are NOT Valid Consideration

  • Past consideration — something already done before the promise was made. You can't bargain for something that already happened.
  • Pre-existing duty — promising to do something you're already legally required to do (e.g., a police officer promising to investigate a crime in exchange for payment).
  • Illusory promise — a "promise" that doesn't actually commit you to anything ("I'll buy your car if I feel like it"). No real obligation = no consideration.
Example — $1 for a House

A sells B a house for $1. Legally sufficient? Yes. Courts don't care about fairness or equal value — $1 is something of legal value, it was bargained for, and the deal was voluntary. The court won't undo it just because it seems wildly unequal.

Example — Unilateral Contract (No Simultaneous Exchange)

"I'll pay you $500 if you find my dog." The promise comes first; the performance (finding the dog) comes later. No simultaneous exchange — but still valid. The performance IS the consideration, and it doesn't have to happen at the same time as the promise.

Trap #1 — "Fair and Reasonable" Is Never the Answer

The exam will offer "fair and reasonable under the circumstances" as a choice. Courts do not police the fairness of bargains. If two parties voluntarily agree to wildly unequal terms, that's their business. The only question is legal sufficiency — not fairness.

Trap #2 — Equal Value Is Not Required

"Of approximately equal value" is a recurring wrong answer. No equivalence requirement exists. A promise to pay $1,000 in exchange for a $5 trinket is valid consideration if both parties agreed to it. The law doesn't rebalance bad deals.

Trap #3 — Simultaneous Exchange Not Required

Unilateral contracts prove this — the promise is made first and performance follows later. Timing of exchange is irrelevant to whether consideration exists. Installment contracts, employment agreements, and credit sales all involve non-simultaneous exchange.

Trap #4 — Past Consideration Is No Consideration

If someone already did something for you and you then promise to pay them for it, that promise is generally unenforceable — the act was not done in exchange for your promise. Example: your neighbor shovels your driveway without being asked; you later promise $50 for it. No contract — the shoveling was already done.

Unilateral Contracts

Business Law

Unilateral Contract

  • Only one party makes a promise
  • The other party accepts by completing the performance — not by promising
  • Contract forms when the act is completed
  • The offeree is never obligated — they can walk away before finishing

Bilateral Contract

  • Both parties make promises
  • Acceptance = a return promise
  • Contract forms when promises are exchanged
  • Both parties are bound from the moment of acceptance
Classic Example — Lost Dog

"I'll pay you $100 if you find my lost dog."

→ Only the offeror makes a promise
→ You don't accept by saying "I'll look for it" — that's not binding
→ You accept by actually finding and returning the dog
→ Contract forms at the moment the dog is returned, not before

Consideration Still Exists on Both Sides

A common misconception: "only one party makes a promise, so only one side gives consideration." Wrong. The offeror's consideration = the promise to pay. The offeree's consideration = the performance itself (time, effort, detriment). Both sides give something — it's just that one side gives a promise and the other gives an act.

Trap #1 — Contract Does NOT Form When Performance Begins

The contract forms when performance is completed, not when it starts. If you start looking for the dog but don't find it, no contract formed and no payment is owed. The exam may offer "when the offeree begins performance" — that's wrong for unilateral contracts.

Trap #2 — No Consideration Is Never Correct

Some questions imply that because only one party promises, there's no consideration. Wrong. Performance is valid consideration — it's a legal detriment to the offeree. Both sides give something; the form is just different (promise vs. act instead of promise vs. promise).

Trap #3 — The Offeree Is Never Obligated to Perform

In a unilateral contract, the offeree makes no promise — they are free to walk away at any point before completing the act. No breach occurs if they stop halfway. Only the offeror is bound (to pay if the act is completed).

Quick ID — Is It Unilateral?

Look for the phrase "I'll pay you if you [do X]" — reward offers, finder's fees, contest prizes. If the only way to accept is by doing something (not by promising), it's unilateral.

Quasi Contract

Business Law

What It Is

A quasi contract is not a real contract — it's a legal remedy courts impose to prevent unjust enrichment. Also called an implied-in-law contract. There is no mutual agreement, no offer and acceptance, no meeting of the minds — the court simply creates the obligation because fairness requires it.

When Courts Impose It

  • No actual contract exists between the parties
  • One party received a benefit at the other's expense
  • Allowing the benefit to stay would be unjust

What the Remedy Looks Like

  • Court orders the enriched party to pay restitution — the value of the benefit received
  • Not based on any agreement — based purely on equity
  • Amount = value of benefit, not expectation damages
Example 1 — Mistaken Payment

You accidentally wire $1,000 to the wrong person. No contract exists. But letting that person keep $1,000 they didn't earn would be unjust enrichment — so a court imposes a quasi contract and orders them to return it.

Example 2 — Emergency Services

A doctor renders emergency care to an unconscious patient. No contract was formed (patient couldn't consent). The patient clearly benefited — a court can impose a quasi contract requiring payment for the reasonable value of services rendered.

Trap #1 — "Implied-in-Law" ≠ "Implied-in-Fact"

An implied-in-fact contract IS a real contract — it's formed through conduct rather than words (e.g., sitting in a barber's chair implies you'll pay for the haircut). An implied-in-law contract (quasi contract) is NOT a real contract — it's a court remedy. The exam loves this distinction. "Implied" alone doesn't tell you which one it is.

Trap #2 — Quasi Contract Is About Fairness, Not Agreement

Courts impose quasi contracts precisely because there is no agreement. If a real contract exists, quasi contract doesn't apply — you use contract law instead. Quasi contract fills the gap when contract law has nothing to enforce.

Offers vs. Advertisements

Business Law

The Default Rule

Advertisements, price lists, catalogs, and newspaper ads are generally not offers — they are invitations to negotiate (invitations to make an offer). The customer who walks in and says "I'd like to buy one" is making the offer; the seller can accept or decline.

When an Ad IS an Offer

An advertisement crosses into offer territory only if it is specific enough to leave nothing open for negotiation:

  • Specific item identified
  • Specific price stated
  • Specific quantity limited
  • Clear who can accept and how

Example of an ad that IS an offer: "1 fur coat, size 14, $25 — first come, first served, Saturday only." Specific enough that nothing is left to negotiate.

— Which cars exactly
— What the actual price was
— Any specific terms of acceptance

Too vague to be an offer. It was an invitation to negotiate. King never had a contractual right to buy — so Harris can't be in breach.

Best defense: the advertisement was not an offer. (A)

The line between an ad (invitation) and an offer is specificity. If an ad names a price, identifies the specific item, limits quantity, and makes clear how to accept — it can be an offer. Option Contracts & Firm Offers

Business Law

The Default Rule — A Promise Alone Doesn't Bind

An offeror's promise to keep an offer open is not enforceable by itself. The offeror can revoke at any time before acceptance, even if they said they'd wait. To lock the offer open, something more is required.

Option Contract — Common Law

  • Offer + consideration paid by the offeree to keep it open
  • Consideration can be anything of legal value — money, a promise, giving up a right
  • Creates a binding obligation: offeror cannot revoke during the option period
  • Applies to any contract (goods, services, real estate)

Merchant's Firm Offer — UCC Only

  • A merchant makes a written, signed promise to keep an offer open
  • No consideration required — writing + merchant status is enough
  • Binding for the stated time, or if no time stated, a reasonable time
  • Maximum: 3 months
  • Only applies to sale of goods (UCC)
Example — Jen & Bob (Becker)

Facts: Jen offers to sell Bob her laptop for $500. Bob asks Jen to keep the offer open until noon the next day. Jen agrees — if Bob promises not to buy another computer before then. Bob makes that promise.

Is Bob's promise valid consideration? Yes.
Bob had no legal obligation to avoid buying another computer. Giving up that right = legal detriment = valid consideration.

Result: an option contract is formed. Jen is legally obligated to keep the offer open until noon.

Note: Jen's mere word alone (Choice A) would NOT have bound her. The consideration from Bob is what creates the binding option.

Trap #1 — A Verbal Promise to Keep Offer Open = Unenforceable

"I give you my word I'll hold the offer open" is not binding under Common Law without consideration. Jen's word alone in the example doesn't create an obligation. The exam will offer this as a correct answer (Choice A) — it's a trap. You need either consideration (option contract) or the merchant's firm offer rules.

Trap #2 — Giving Up a Right IS Valid Consideration

Bob didn't pay money — he just promised not to buy another computer. That counts. Refraining from doing something you have a legal right to do is valid consideration. The exam will suggest Bob gave nothing of value — wrong. A legal detriment (giving up a right) is just as good as paying cash.

Trap #3 — Merchant's Firm Offer Needs No Consideration But Must Be Written

Under UCC, a merchant can bind themselves to hold an offer open just by putting it in writing — no payment from the other side needed. But it must be in writing and signed. A merchant's oral promise to hold an offer open is still unenforceable without consideration. Writing is the substitute for consideration here.

Trap #4 — Merchant's Firm Offer Caps at 3 Months

Even if the written offer says "I'll hold this open for 6 months," the maximum enforceable period under UCC is 3 months. After 3 months, the offeror can revoke. If the parties want a longer lock-up, they need an option contract with consideration.

Offer Termination

Business Law

Termination by Operation of Law

Automatic — no notice required to either party

  • Death or incapacity of either party
  • Destruction of the subject matter
  • Supervening illegality — subject matter becomes illegal after the offer is made

Termination by Act of the Parties

  • Rejection by the offeree
  • Counteroffer (treated as rejection under Common Law)
  • Revocation by the offeror (before acceptance)
  • Lapse of time — stated expiration, or reasonable time if none stated
Example — Opal & Larkin (Becker)

Facts: Opal offers in writing to sell Larkin land for $300,000. Opal dies before Larkin accepts.

Death of the offeror = automatic termination by operation of law.
No notice to Larkin required. Even if Larkin has no idea Opal died, the offer is gone.
Answer: offer automatically terminates prior to acceptance. (A)

Critical Exception — Option Contracts Survive Death

If Larkin had paid consideration to keep the offer open (forming an option contract), Opal's death would not terminate it. The option is an irrevocable right Larkin already purchased — it survives the offeror's death and binds the estate.

Trap #1 — No Notice Required for Operation-of-Law Terminations

When an offer terminates by operation of law (death, destruction, illegality), the offeree does not need to be notified. The termination is automatic the moment the triggering event occurs. The exam will offer "only if Larkin received notice" — that's wrong. Larkin's knowledge is irrelevant.

Trap #2 — Death Before Acceptance vs. Death After Acceptance

Death terminates an offer — but if acceptance already occurred before death, a contract exists and the estate may be bound. The timing question is: did death happen before or after acceptance? Before acceptance → offer is gone. After acceptance → contract survives.

Trap #3 — Counteroffer Kills the Original Offer

Under Common Law's mirror image rule, a counteroffer is treated as a rejection of the original offer. Once Larkin responds "I'll pay $280,000," Opal's $300,000 offer is dead — Larkin can't later come back and accept it. This is why the mirror image rule matters: any deviation = new offer, old offer gone.

Gifted Property Basis

Property Taxation

Cash Gifts — Never Taxable to Recipient

Dad gives you $50,000 cash.

You: $0 tax
Dad: $0 deduction
Not taxable income to recipient — ever.

Exam shortcut: Gifts are never income to the recipient.

Gift Tax — Donor Pays, Not Recipient

Dad gives you $100,000. Who owes gift tax?

❌ Recipient
✅ Donor (possibly — depends on annual exclusion)

Becker loves: "Who is responsible for gift tax?" Answer: always the donor.

Carryover Basis (FMV > Donor Basis)

Mom bought stock for $10,000. FMV = $50,000. She gifts it to you.

Your basis: $10,000 (NOT $50,000)
You sell for $70,000:
Gain = $70,000 − $10,000 = $60,000

IRS preserved all the appreciation. The donor's low basis carries over to you.

Holding Period Tacks On

Mom owned stock 5 years, gifts it to you. You sell the next day.

Your holding period: 5 years + 1 day
✅ Long-term capital gain

The donor's holding period tacks onto yours. Very testable.

The Dual-Basis Rule — Most Important (FMV < Donor Basis)

Property has gone down in value before the gift. Mom's basis = $100,000, FMV at gift date = $80,000. This is the one students miss.

You sell for Basis used Result
$120,000 (above both) Donor basis: $100,000 Gain = $20,000

$90,000 (between) N/A No gain, no loss

Sale price between $80K and $100K = phantom zone — no gain, no loss recognized. CPA exam favorite.

Why the phantom zone exists: The IRS is blocking two things simultaneously.
① Using donor basis ($100K) → would show a $10K loss on a $90K sale → IRS says no, you can't inherit the donor's built-in loss.
② Using FMV ($80K) → would show a $10K gain on a $90K sale → IRS says no, the recipient didn't actually gain anything relative to the gifted value.
Neither basis works cleanly → IRS throws up its hands → no gain, no loss.
Don't try to make this feel intuitive. Memorize the three zones and move on.

Personal → Business Conversion Basis

Property Taxation

The Core Rule

Depreciation basis = Lower of adjusted basis (cost) OR FMV at conversion date.

Why: The IRS won't let you deduct personal losses. If your asset lost value while it was personal property, that decline happened in your personal life — not the business. Allowing you to depreciate the original cost would let you write off that personal loss, which they don't allow.

The IRS also won't let you step up basis when FMV rises. Lower always wins — both gains and losses from personal life are ignored.

Example 1 — Value Dropped
Cost: $2,000
FMV at conversion: $1,200
Lower = FMV
Depreciation basis = $1,200

The $800 personal decline is gone — IRS won't let you recover it through depreciation.

Example 2 — Value Increased
Cost: $1,500
FMV at conversion: $1,800
Lower = Cost
Depreciation basis = $1,500

Even though it appreciated, IRS won't step up basis. Still use the lower cost.

Very Testable Example — Desk
Bought desk for: $10,000
FMV at conversion: $7,000
Depreciation basis = $7,000 (not $10,000)

Greller bought 100 shares of Arden Corp. on Jan 1, Year 1 for $10,000. On Jan 1, Year 3, Arden declared a 2-for-1 stock split (FMV $120/share pre-split, $62/share post-split). On Feb 1, Year 3, Greller sold the 100 split shares at $65/share. What is the basis of the 100 shares sold?

A. $5,000 ✓
B. $6,500
C. $6,000
D. $6,200
Why A: Total basis stays $10,000. Split creates 200 shares → $10,000 ÷ 200 = $50/share.
Greller sells 100 of those shares → 100 × $50 = $5,000 basis.
FMV ($65) is irrelevant to basis — it's used for gain/loss calc, not basis. Distractors B/C/D all use FMV, which is the trap.

A shareholder held 100 shares with a $4,000 total basis. During the year they received a $300 cash dividend and a 10% stock dividend. What is the cost basis per share at year end?

A. $34
B. $36 ✓
C. $37
D. $39
Why B: Stock dividend: 100 × 10% = 10 new shares → now 110 shares total.
Total basis stays $4,000 → $4,000 ÷ 110 = $36.36 ≈ $36/share.
Cash dividend ($300) is ignored — it doesn't touch stock basis at all. Classic distractor trap.

Berk bought land with a cash payment of $40,000 and a mortgage of $50,000. He also paid $200 for a title insurance policy. What is Berk's basis in the land?

A. $40,000
B. $40,200
C. $90,200 ✓
D. $90,000
Why C: Basis = everything paid to acquire the property.
$40,000 cash + $50,000 mortgage (debt assumed = part of cost) + $200 title policy = $90,200.
Trap: thinking only cash counts (A/B). Debt assumed is always included in basis.
