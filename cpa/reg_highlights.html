Individual Taxation
§165 — Personal Casualty Loss
Federally Declared Disaster — Deductible Loss
Personal-use property only — Schedule A itemized deduction
01
1
Determine the loss amount
Take the lesser of: adjusted basis or decline in FMV (FMV before − FMV after).
2
Subtract insurance / reimbursements
Reduce by any insurance proceeds or other compensation received.
3
Apply the $100 per-event floor
Subtract $100 for each separate casualty event.
4
Apply the 10% of AGI limitation
Only the amount exceeding 10% × AGI is deductible. If the result is negative, the deduction is zero.
Formula
Loss = max(0, [min(Adj. Basis, Decline in FMV) − Insurance]
Loss =        − $100 − 10% × AGI )
Worked Example
Adjusted basis $36,500 FMV before disaster$36,500 FMV after disaster $21,500 Insurance received $0 AGI $40,000
Step 1 — lesser of $36,500 or decline ($36,500 − $21,500) $15,000
Step 2 — subtract insurance $15,000 − $0 = $15,000
Step 3 — subtract $100 floor $15,000 − $100 = $14,900
Step 4 — subtract 10% × $40,000 AGI $14,900 − $4,000 = $10,900
Deductible casualty loss (Schedule A) $10,900
Exam Traps
▶ Federally declared disaster only. Post-2017 TCJA: personal casualty losses are only deductible if the property is in a presidentially declared disaster area. Other personal casualty losses are suspended through 2025.
▶ Lesser of, not the larger. If basis ($36,500) > decline in FMV ($15,000), you use $15,000. The exam loves giving you a higher basis to tempt the wrong number.
▶ $100 comes before the 10% test. Apply the floor first, then subtract 10% AGI — not the other way around.
▶ Business-use property is different. The $100 floor and 10%-of-AGI limit do NOT apply to business or income-producing property. Business casualty losses are fully deductible on Schedule C / Form 4797.
Business Taxation
§179 — Expensing Election
Section 179 Phase-Out for Excess Property
Dollar-for-dollar reduction when total qualified property exceeds the threshold
02
1
Calculate excess property placed in service
Total qualified property placed in service − phase-out threshold. If zero or negative, no reduction applies.
2
Reduce the §179 maximum dollar-for-dollar
Subtract the excess from the maximum §179 deduction. The reduction is $1 for every $1 of excess — not a percentage.
3
Floor at zero — no negative deduction
If excess ≥ maximum, the §179 deduction is fully phased out ($0). Any remaining basis is depreciated normally under MACRS.
Formula (2026 limits)
Excess = Total Property − $4,090,000 (phase-out threshold)
§179 Deduction = max(0, $2,560,000 − Excess )
Worked Example — Martin Corporation (2026)
Property placed in service$4,590,000 Phase-out threshold (2026)$4,090,000 §179 maximum (2026) $2,560,000
Step 1 — excess over threshold $4,590,000 − $4,090,000 = $500,000
Step 2 — reduce maximum dollar-for-dollar $2,560,000 − $500,000 = $2,060,000
§179 deduction allowed $2,060,000
Exam Traps
▶ $500K distractor. The exam often lists the excess amount ($500,000) as an answer choice. That's the reduction, not the deduction.
▶ Dollar-for-dollar, not proportional. Every dollar of excess above the threshold cuts the deduction by exactly $1 — there's no percentage involved.
▶ Also limited by taxable income. Even after the phase-out, §179 cannot exceed the entity's taxable income from active business. Unused §179 carries forward indefinitely (but the phase-out recalculates each year).
▶ Limits adjust annually for inflation. Memorize the structure ($max − excess), not the dollar amounts — the exam will always give you the year's figures.
Individual Taxation — Education Credits
§25A — Education Credits
American Opportunity Credit & Lifetime Learning Credit
Two credits, same return — but never for the same student in the same year
03
1
American Opportunity Credit (AOC) — per student
100% of first $2,000 of qualified expenses + 25% of next $2,000. Max $2,500 per eligible student. First 4 years of post-secondary only; student must be enrolled at least half-time for at least one academic period.
2
Lifetime Learning Credit (LLC) — per return
20% of qualified expenses up to $10,000. Max $2,000 per return (not per student). No half-time enrollment requirement — any course counts.
3
Check eligibility — AOC requirements are stricter
If a student doesn't qualify for AOC (e.g., not half-time, or past year 4), their expenses fall to LLC instead. Both credits can appear on the same return for different students.
AOC Formula — per eligible student
AOC = (min(expenses, $2,000) × 100%) + (min(max(0, expenses − $2,000), $2,000) × 25%)
LLC Formula — per return
LLC = min(qualified expenses, $10,000) × 20%    [max $2,000 per return]
Worked Example — Jermaine & Keesha (MFJ, MAGI $50,000)
Devona (full-time, year 1)$3,600 → AOC Arethia (not half-time, yr 4)$4,250 → LLC
Devona AOC — first $2,000 × 100% $2,000
Devona AOC — next $1,600 × 25% $400
AOC total (Devona) $2,400
Arethia LLC — $4,250 × 20% $850
LLC total (per return) $850
Exam Traps
▶ Arethia can't use AOC — not half-time. The exam tells you she won't be enrolled at least half-time. That one fact disqualifies AOC entirely; her expenses fall to LLC.
▶ AOC is 100% + 25%, not 100% flat. $3,600 → $2,000 at 100% = $2,000, then $1,600 at 25% = $400 → total $2,400. Not $2,500 (max) and not $3,600 × 100%.
▶ LLC is 20% of actual expenses, not 20% of $10,000. The $10,000 is a cap on eligible expenses, not a guaranteed base. $4,250 × 20% = $850, not $2,000.
▶ LLC is per return, not per student. Even if both children qualified for LLC, the combined credit is still capped at $2,000 total — not $2,000 each.
Business Taxation — C Corporations
§170 — C Corp Charitable Contributions
C Corporation Charitable Deduction — Floor & Ceiling
Two-step limit: 1% floor reduces total; 10% ceiling caps the deduction
04
1
Compute Adjusted Taxable Income (ATI)
Start with taxable income before the charitable deduction, then add back: the dividends-received deduction (DRD) and any capital loss carryback.
2
Find the floor and ceiling
Floor = 1% × ATI — this amount is non-deductible (reduces available deduction).
Ceiling = 10% × ATI — hard cap on total deductible contributions.
3
Sum current-year + carryover contributions
Add current year actual contributions and any carryover from prior years (5-year carryforward).
4
Apply floor, then cap at ceiling
Subtract the 1% floor from total contributions. Then take the lesser of that result or the 10% ceiling. The amount cut by the 1% floor is permanently lost — it cannot carry forward.
Step 1 — ATI
ATI = Taxable income (before charit. deduction) + DRD + Capital loss carryback
Step 4 — Deduction
Deduction = min( [Current contrib. + Carryover] − 1% × ATI ,   10% × ATI )
Worked Example — Radon Corporation
Taxable income (before charit.)$1,400,000 DRD included above $100,000 Current year contributions $120,000 Carryover from prior year $25,000
Step 1 — ATI: $1,400,000 + $100,000 DRD $1,500,000
Step 2 — 1% floor: $1,500,000 × 1% $15,000
Step 2 — 10% ceiling: $1,500,000 × 10% $150,000
Step 3 — total contributions: $120,000 + $25,000 $145,000
Step 4 — subtract 1% floor: $145,000 − $15,000 $130,000
$130,000 < $150,000 ceiling — full amount allowed ✓
Charitable deduction for current year $130,000
Exam Traps
▶ Must add back DRD to get ATI. The exam gives you taxable income that already includes the DRD deduction — you have to reverse it. Forgetting this understates ATI, understates both the floor and ceiling.
▶ $150,000 is the ceiling, not the answer. You can never deduct more than contributions actually paid + carryovers. Radon only contributed $145,000 total, so $150,000 is off the table regardless.
▶ $145,000 ignores the 1% floor. The floor is not optional — it always reduces the deductible amount first.
▶ 1% disallowance is permanent — cannot carry forward. The $15,000 cut by the floor is gone. However, amounts exceeding the 10% ceiling CAN carry forward up to 5 years.
Business Taxation — Depreciation
MACRS — Depreciation Conventions
Which Convention Applies? Half-Year vs. Mid-Quarter vs. Mid-Month
Two separate tracks: real property always mid-month; personal property depends on the 40% test
05
1
Real property → always mid-month
Buildings and structural components use the mid-month convention — no exceptions, no test needed. Land is never depreciable and is excluded from all calculations.
2
Personal property → run the 40% test
Default is half-year convention. But if more than 40% of total personal property basis placed in service during the year falls in Q4 (Oct–Dec), the mid-quarter convention applies to all personal property placed in service that year.
3
Mid-quarter: if triggered, it applies to everything
Once the 40% threshold is crossed, every piece of personal property placed in service that year uses mid-quarter — even items placed in Q1.
40% Test — triggers mid-quarter for personal property
Q4 % = Personal property placed in service Q4 ÷ Total personal property placed in service (yr)
If Q4 % > 40% → use mid-quarter for all personal property   |  else → half-year
Worked Example — Star Corporation
Building (March) — real property $400,000 Land (March) — not depreciable $150,000 Equipment (Nov 15) — personal prop. $80,000
Building → real property → mid-month (always) Mid-Month ✓
40% test — Q4 personal property: $80,000 $80,000
Total personal property placed in service: $80,000 $80,000
Q4 % = $80,000 ÷ $80,000 = 100% > 40% Triggered
Equipment → mid-quarter convention applies Mid-Quarter ✓
Exam Traps
▶ Land and buildings are excluded from the 40% test. Only personal property (equipment, furniture, vehicles) goes into the numerator and denominator. The $400K building and $150K land are irrelevant to the test.
▶ Buildings never get half-year or mid-quarter. Real property is always mid-month — the exam frequently offers half-year for the building as a wrong answer choice.
▶ Mid-quarter infects all personal property for the year. If one late-year purchase triggers it, everything — including a piece of equipment bought in January — must use mid-quarter for that year.
▶ There is no "full-year convention." It doesn't exist under MACRS. If you see it as an answer choice, it's always wrong.
Individual Taxation — Itemized Deductions
§213 — Medical Expense Deduction
Schedule A Medical Expenses — Qualified Items & 7.5% AGI Floor
Subtract reimbursements first, then apply the floor — order matters
06
1
Sum only qualified medical expenses
Include: insurance premiums, deductibles, hospital costs, prescription drugs & co-pays, doctor/dentist visits, prescription eyeglasses, transportation to medical care.
Exclude: vitamins/supplements for general health, health club dues, cosmetic procedures (unless reconstructive).
2
Subtract insurance reimbursements
Reduce qualified expenses by any insurance reimbursements before applying the AGI floor. This step trips up most exam takers.
3
Apply the 7.5% of AGI floor
Only the amount exceeding 7.5% × AGI is deductible. Applies to all taxpayers regardless of age (TCJA made 7.5% permanent).
Formula
Deduction = max(0, [Qualified expenses − Reimbursements] − 7.5% × AGI )
Worked Example — Jan Thomas (AGI $80,000)
Expense Amount Qualified?
Medical insurance premiums$8,000✓
Deductible paid$1,000✓
Hospital / ER costs$2,000✓
Vitamins — general health$500✗
Prescription eyeglasses$200✓
Doctor & dentist visits$700✓
Health club dues — general health$1,200✗
Prescription drug co-pays$300✓
Transportation to doctor$100✓
Insurance reimbursement($1,500)—
Step 1 — qualified expenses only (excl. vitamins & health club) $12,300
Step 2 — subtract reimbursement: $12,300 − $1,500 $10,800
Step 3 — 7.5% floor: $80,000 × 7.5% $6,000
$10,800 − $6,000 floor $4,800
Deductible medical expenses (Schedule A) $4,800
Exam Traps
▶ Vitamins and health club dues are never deductible. "General health" = nondeductible. The expense must treat or diagnose a specific medical condition. $6,500 is the distractor that forgets to exclude these two.
▶ Subtract reimbursements before the floor, not after. $6,300 is the distractor that skips the reimbursement reduction. Reimbursements always reduce gross qualified expenses first.
▶ 7.5% floor is permanent for all ages (post-TCJA). Pre-2021 it was 10% for under-65 taxpayers. The exam may test the old 10% rule — current law is 7.5% regardless of age.
▶ Transportation to medical care is qualified. Easy to overlook — the $100 counts. Mileage to/from required medical appointments is a deductible medical expense.
Individual Taxation — Core Formula
Individual Income Tax — Formula Order
Gross Income → Taxable Income: The Correct Sequence
Four deduction layers in a fixed order — QBI and Additional deductions always come last
07
The Individual Income Tax Formula
Gross Income
−  Adjustments  (above-the-line deductions)
=  Adjusted Gross Income (AGI)
−  Standard deduction or Itemized deductions
−  QBI deduction  (§199A — 20% of qualified business income)
−  Additional deductions  (e.g., qualified disability trust deduction)
=  Taxable Income
Order to Memorize
1 — Adjustments → gets you to AGI
2 — Standard or Itemized deduction → from AGI
3 — QBI deduction (§199A) → after Std/Itemized
4 — Additional deductions → last, → Taxable Income
Exam Traps
▶ QBI deduction is NOT an adjustment and NOT itemized. It's its own deduction layer after Std/Itemized but before Additional deductions. Misplacing it is the most common wrong answer.
▶ Adjustments always come first — they reduce gross income to arrive at AGI, which then becomes the base for the 7.5% medical floor, 2% misc. floors, and other AGI-dependent calculations.
▶ Standard vs. Itemized — choose one, not both. The taxpayer takes whichever is greater. The QBI and Additional deductions apply regardless of which is chosen.

Business Law — UCC Article 9
UCC Article 9 — Secured Transactions
Attachment & Perfection of a Security Interest
Attachment comes first — perfection without attachment is impossible
09
Step 1 — Attachment (3 Requirements — ALL must be met)
A
Agreement
Written or authenticated security agreement signed by the debtor describing the collateral.
B
Value given
Creditor must give value — e.g., extend a loan or extend credit.
C
Debtor's rights in collateral
Debtor must have rights or the power to transfer rights in the collateral.
Step 2 — Perfection (5 Methods)
1
Filing a Financing Statement
Used for most collateral, especially intangibles (accounts receivable, general intangibles). Must include debtor name & address, secured party name & address, and collateral description. Filed at state level. Can file before attachment — perfection only begins at attachment.
2
Possession
Tangible collateral only (goods, negotiable documents, certificated securities). Creditor physically holds the collateral. Perfects instantly while possession lasts. Cannot use for intangibles (accounts receivable, deposit accounts).
3
Control
Used for investment property (stocks, bonds, securities accounts) and deposit accounts. Creditor obtains legal control — e.g., debtor authorizes broker to comply with creditor's instructions. No filing or possession needed.
4
Automatic Perfection
No filing or possession required. Applies to: (1) PMSI in consumer goods — perfected upon attachment; (2) small-scale assignments of accounts receivable. Does NOT apply to large-scale account assignments or equipment PMSIs.
5
Temporary Perfection
20-day grace period for proceeds after collateral is sold. 4-month grace period if debtor moves to a new state. Must file in new jurisdiction before grace period expires.
Perfection Method by Collateral Type
Collateral Type Method(s) Notes
Tangible Goods Filing, Possession Possession perfects instantly while held
Accounts Receivable Filing, Automatic (small-scale only) Cannot perfect by possession
Investment Property Filing, Control Control is preferred & superior method
Consumer Goods (PMSI) Automatic Perfected upon attachment — no filing needed
Deposit Accounts Control only Cannot perfect by filing or possession
Proceeds Temporary (20-day grace) Must perfect in new collateral after 20 days
PMSI — Purchase Money Security Interest Deep Dive

A PMSI arises when a creditor finances the debtor's purchase of the specific collateral. It gets super-priority over earlier-perfected interests — even a creditor who filed first can be leapfrogged by a PMSI creditor who perfects in time.

Collateral How to Perfect Deadline for Priority Extra Notice Required?
Consumer Goods Automatic upon attachment No filing needed No
Equipment File financing statement Within 20 days of delivery No
Inventory File financing statement Before debtor gets possession Yes — notify prior secured parties

Priority rule: Normally first to file or perfect wins. A properly perfected PMSI is the exception — it jumps the line over earlier-perfected interests. The 20-day grace period for equipment "relates back" to attachment, giving the PMSI creditor retroactive priority.

Exam Traps
▶ Filing before attachment is allowed — but perfection doesn't start until attachment. You can file the financing statement early, but the clock on perfection only begins once all 3 attachment requirements are met.
▶ PMSI automatic perfection is consumer goods only — equipment and inventory both require filing. Equipment: file within 20 days. Inventory: file AND notify prior secured parties before debtor takes possession. Missing either step loses the super-priority.
▶ Inventory PMSI requires advance notice to prior secured parties. Equipment PMSI does not. This distinction is a classic exam question — inventory is stricter because it turns over fast and affects existing lenders more directly.
▶ Deposit accounts can only be perfected by control. Filing and possession are both invalid. The exam will offer filing as a trap answer for deposit accounts.
▶ Grace periods: 20 days for PMSI equipment & proceeds, 4 months for interstate moves. Don't confuse the two — the exam tests these numbers against each other.
Business Taxation — §197 Intangibles
§197 — Intangible Amortization
§197 Purchased Intangibles — Always 180 Months
Ignore the actual useful life — 180 months applies regardless, full-month convention
10
1
Identify §197 intangibles
Goodwill, going concern value, patents, copyrights, franchises, trademarks, trade names, covenants not-to-compete, customer lists — when acquired as part of a business purchase. Self-created intangibles are NOT §197.
2
Pool all §197 intangibles from the same acquisition
Every §197 intangible acquired in the same business purchase is grouped together. One total basis, one 180-month clock — not separate schedules per asset.
3
Amortize over 180 months — full-month convention
Divide total basis by 180 to get monthly amortization. Count full months starting in the month of acquisition — even if purchased on the last day of the month.
Formula
Annual Amortization = ( Total §197 basis ÷ 180 ) × Months in service
Convention: full-month — count from month of acquisition, not mid-month or half-year
Worked Example — Fran acquires Chuck's business (January 2)
Patent (10-yr remaining life) $60,000 Covenant not-to-compete (5-yr) $30,000 Goodwill (excess purchase price) $45,000 Total §197 basis $135,000
Monthly amortization: $135,000 ÷ 180 months $750/mo
Months in service: January–December (acquired Jan 2) × 12 months
§197 amortization deduction for the year $9,000
Exam Traps
▶ Never use the actual useful life. The patent has 10 years left, the covenant is 5 years — irrelevant. §197 overrides both. Using those lives ($6,000 + $6,000 = $12,000) is the most common wrong answer on this topic.
▶ Goodwill IS amortizable for tax purposes under §197. Unlike GAAP (where goodwill is tested for impairment, not amortized), tax law allows full amortization over 180 months. Skipping goodwill gets you $6,000 — another distractor.
▶ Full-month convention — not half-year, not mid-month. If acquired January 2, you get 12 full months. MACRS conventions do not apply to §197 intangibles.
▶ Must be acquired in a business purchase — self-created intangibles don't qualify. A patent you developed internally is not §197. Only purchased intangibles acquired alongside a going business get the 180-month treatment.

Business Taxation — Net Operating Losses
NOL — Net Operating Loss
NOL Generation, Carryforward & 80% Limitation
C corps claim at entity level — pass-throughs (S corps, partnerships) do not
12
1
Generate the NOL
NOL arises when allowable deductions exceed gross income. For C corps: Gross Income − Expenses − Charitable contributions − DRD = negative taxable income → that negative amount is the NOL.
2
Carry it forward (post-2017 TCJA rules)
NOLs generated in 2021+ carry forward indefinitely. No carryback. In the carryforward year, the NOL deduction is capped at 80% of taxable income — the corporation can never be reduced below zero using the NOL alone.
3
Apply the deduction — max allowed vs. NOL available
Deduct the lesser of: the available NOL carryforward, or 80% of current-year taxable income. Any unused NOL continues to carry forward.
NOL Rules by Year Generated
NOL Year Carryback Carryforward TI Limit
≤ 2017 2 years 20 years 100%
2018–2020 (CARES Act) 5 years (optional) Indefinite 100%
2021+ (TCJA) None Indefinite 80%
NOL Deduction Allowed (2021+ rules)
NOL Deduction = min( Available NOL carryforward ,   80% × Current-year taxable income )
Worked Example — C Corporation (2026/2027)
Year 1 (2026) — NOL Generated
Gross income $300,000 Business expenses ($400,000) Charitable contributions($10,000) DRD ($5,000) Taxable income (NOL) ($115,000)
Year 2 (2027) — NOL Applied
Taxable income before NOL $150,000
80% limit: $150,000 × 80% = $120,000 $120,000 max
NOL available ($115,000) < $120,000 limit → full NOL used ($115,000)
Year 2 taxable income after NOL $35,000
Exam Traps
▶ S corps and partnerships do NOT claim NOLs at the entity level. Losses pass through to owners who apply them on their individual returns (subject to basis, at-risk, and passive activity rules). Only C corps, individuals, estates, and trusts claim NOLs directly.
▶ Post-2020 NOLs: 80% cap, no carryback, indefinite forward. The 80% cap means you can never use an NOL to reduce taxable income all the way to zero — there's always a minimum taxable floor.
▶ 2018–2020 NOLs had a 5-year carryback option (CARES Act) and no 80% cap. Don't confuse these with post-2020 rules — the exam may specify the year to force you to apply the right set of rules.
▶ 20-year carryforward = pre-2018 NOL distractor. The exam loves listing "20 years" as an answer choice — that's only for NOLs generated in 2017 or earlier. Post-2017 NOLs carry forward indefinitely.
Business Taxation — Partnerships
Partnerships — Form 1065 / Schedule K-1
Partnership Pass-Through Mechanics, Outside Basis & Separately Stated Items
Highest-yield REG topic — partnership reports, partners pay
13
⭐ Tier 1 — Must Know
1
Pass-through entity — no entity-level federal tax
Partnership files Form 1065 (information return only — no tax paid at entity level). Each partner receives a Schedule K-1 and reports their allocable share on their own return. Memory: Partnership reports. Partners pay.
2
Separately stated items — they keep their character on the K-1
Reported separately because every partner's tax situation is different (different AGIs, capital loss carryovers, charitable limits, passive loss limitations).
Separately stated: capital gains/losses · charitable contributions · §179 deduction · tax-exempt interest · dividend income · foreign taxes.
NOT separately stated: ordinary business income — netted at the entity level.
3
Guaranteed payments — "salary," not "distribution"
Paid regardless of whether the partnership has income. Deducted by the partnership (reduces ordinary income on Form 1065). Ordinary income to the partner — always taxable, always reported on K-1. Not a distribution.
4
Outside basis — what increases and decreases it
Increases (+): capital contributions · share of taxable income · share of tax-exempt income · increases in share of partnership liabilities.
Decreases (−): cash distributions · share of losses · nondeductible expenses · decreases in share of partnership liabilities.
5
Cash distributions — tax-free until basis hits zero
Each cash distribution reduces outside basis dollar-for-dollar. If cash distributed exceeds outside basis → the excess is recognized as a capital gain. Outside basis cannot go below zero.
6
Liabilities affect outside basis
A partner's share of partnership liabilities is included in outside basis. Increase in share → basis goes up (treated like a contribution). Decrease in share → basis goes down (treated like a distribution). This is why partners can sometimes deduct losses beyond their cash investment.
⭐ Tier 2 — Know Well
A
Inside basis vs. outside basis
Inside basis = the partnership's own basis in its assets.
Outside basis = a partner's basis in their partnership interest.
They start equal at formation but diverge as contributions, distributions, and debt allocations change over time.
B
Formation — generally no gain or loss recognized
Contributing property for a partnership interest is generally tax-free. The partnership takes a carryover basis in the contributed asset. A contributed capital asset's holding period carries over to the partnership; cash contributions start a new holding period.
C
Income flows through when earned — not when distributed
A partner reports their K-1 share of partnership income in the year the partnership earns it, even if no cash was distributed. Taxable in the year of K-1, period.
Outside Basis Waterfall
  Beginning outside basis
+  Capital contributions
+  Share of taxable income (incl. separately stated income)
+  Share of tax-exempt income
+  Increase in share of partnership liabilities
−  Cash distributions
−  Share of losses (incl. separately stated losses)
−  Nondeductible expenses
−  Decrease in share of partnership liabilities

Exam Traps & Common Confusions
▶ Partnership pays no federal income tax. Only C corporations pay entity-level tax. Partnerships and S corps are pure pass-throughs — the tax obligation sits entirely with the partners/shareholders on their own returns.
▶ Guaranteed payment ≠ distribution. A guaranteed payment is always ordinary income to the recipient and always deducted by the partnership. A distribution reduces basis and is usually tax-free. The exam loves asking you to classify one as the other.
▶ Tax-exempt income increases outside basis. Even though it is never taxed, tax-exempt income flows through and increases each partner's outside basis. Skipping this step understates basis and artificially limits loss deductions — a common calculation error.
▶ Cash distribution exceeding basis → capital gain, not ordinary income. The excess is capital in character. The exam will tempt you to say "ordinary income" because it came from a partnership — that's wrong.
▶ Income is taxed when earned, not when distributed. If the partnership earns $50K in Year 1 and distributes it in Year 2, the partner pays tax in Year 1. The Year 2 cash is a return of already-taxed basis — generally tax-free.
▶ Unlike S corps, partnership liabilities flow into partner outside basis. An increase in a partner's share of debt increases basis dollar-for-dollar. S corp debt at the entity level does NOT increase shareholder basis — only direct loans from the shareholder do. Classic cross-topic trap.

Exam Traps
▶ Land is never depreciable — always separate it from the building. If an exam question gives you a combined purchase price, you must exclude the land portion before computing any depreciation. Depreciating land cost is always a wrong answer.
▶ Buildings are never §179 eligible. §179 applies only to personal property (equipment, furniture, computers, etc.). A building — even one used 100% for business — cannot be expensed under §179. Residential rental = 27.5 years, commercial = 39 years, no shortcuts.
▶ Half-year convention applies no matter when in the year the asset was purchased. A machine bought December 29 still gets half a year's depreciation in Year 1 — not a full year, not a fraction of a month. (Unless the mid-quarter convention is triggered.)
▶ Escalators and elevators cannot be §179 expensed. They are structural components of a building and must be depreciated over 39 years. Don't let the fact that they're "equipment" fool you — their connection to the building structure controls.
▶ §179 carryforward vs. bonus — they work differently. Unused §179 (limited by business income) carries forward indefinitely. Unused bonus depreciation (no income limit) does not carry forward — it converts to a higher NOL in the same year instead.
Individual Taxation — §199A QBI Deduction
§199A — Qualified Business Income Deduction
QBI Deduction: 20% of Pass-Through Business Income
Below-the-line deduction — reduces taxable income, not AGI · C corps and employees don't qualify
15
1
What it is — 20% of qualified business income, below the line
Owners of pass-through businesses can deduct up to 20% of their qualified business income (QBI). This is a below-the-line deduction — it reduces taxable income, but does NOT reduce AGI. It comes after the standard/itemized deduction and before Additional deductions in the individual tax formula.
2
Who qualifies — pass-through owners only
✅ Sole proprietors (Schedule C)
✅ Partners in a partnership (based on K-1 ordinary income)
✅ S corporation shareholders (based on K-1 ordinary income)
✅ Some trusts and estates
Each partner/shareholder computes their own QBI deduction using their K-1 share — this is why partnerships separately state income.
3
Who does NOT qualify
❌ C corporations — they pay entity-level corporate tax; no pass-through deduction applies.
❌ Employees (W-2 wages) — a salary from an employer is never QBI, even if the employer is a pass-through. You must be an owner, not just a worker.
4
What counts as QBI — and what doesn't
Counts as QBI: ordinary business income from the pass-through.
Excluded from QBI: capital gains · dividend income · interest income (unless from the business itself) · W-2 wages received as an employee · guaranteed payments to partners.
5
High-income phaseout — SSTB rules (know the concept)
At higher taxable income, the IRS phases out or limits the deduction for Specified Service Trade or Business (SSTB) owners. SSTBs include: doctors · lawyers · CPAs · consultants · financial advisors. For REG, know that SSTBs can lose some or all of the deduction once taxable income exceeds the phaseout range. The exam usually tells you to ignore wage/property/SSTB limits unless specifically testing them.
Basic QBI Deduction (no limitations)
QBI Deduction = 20% × Qualified Business Income
Placed in individual tax formula: Taxable Income = AGI − Std/Itemized − QBI Deduction − Additional
At High Income — SSTB Phase-Out (concept level)
If SSTB and taxable income > phaseout threshold: QBI Deduction → reduced or $0
Non-SSTB: wage & property limitations may apply at high income — exam will specify
Three Scenarios — Who Gets the Deduction?
Scenario A — Sole proprietor, $100K profit $20,000
20% × $100,000 = $20,000 QBI deduction. Taxable income = $80,000.
Scenario B — Sarah, consulting partnership, $150K K-1 income $30,000
20% × $150,000 = $30,000 QBI deduction (assuming no SSTB phaseout applies).
Scenario C — Employee, $160,000 W-2 salary from Intuit $0
W-2 wages from an employer are never QBI. No ownership interest = no deduction.

Exam Traps
▶ QBI is below the line — it does NOT reduce AGI. The deduction comes after standard/itemized deductions in the tax formula. AGI-based calculations (7.5% medical floor, passive loss rules, etc.) use AGI before the QBI deduction.
▶ Guaranteed payments to partners are NOT QBI. Partners deduct them for §199A purposes — they are excluded from the QBI calculation. This is a specific cross-topic trap: guaranteed payments are ordinary income to the partner on the K-1, but that doesn't make them eligible for the 20% deduction.
▶ Capital gains, dividends, and interest are excluded from QBI. Even if the pass-through entity generates these items, they don't count toward the QBI base. Only ordinary business income qualifies.
▶ C corporations get no QBI deduction — they pay corporate tax instead. The deduction exists specifically to give pass-through businesses parity with C corps after the 2017 TCJA rate cuts. The question will sometimes ask whether a C corp owner gets the deduction — they don't.
▶ SSTBs (doctors, lawyers, CPAs, consultants, financial advisors) can lose the deduction at high income. Below the phaseout range, they get the full 20% just like anyone else. Above it, the deduction shrinks and eventually disappears. The exam usually specifies "ignore limitations" or "no limitations apply" — when it does, just calculate 20% × QBI.
